# Major-Family Evidence: Law-Oriented Pathways

Scope: pre-law interest broadly — this family does not correspond to a college major at all,
which is itself the first and most important finding.

## There is no "pre-law major" — an explicit, important finding for this family specifically

Pre-law advising sources are consistent and explicit: **law schools do not require or particularly
reward a specific undergraduate major.** Instead, the skills that transfer — analytical writing,
critical reasoning, oral communication — are commonly built through English, political science,
philosophy, economics, or several other humanities/social-science paths. (Source: multiple
university pre-law advising office pages, describing standard, non-controversial guidance — high
confidence as consensus, medium confidence that any single page is "the" official position since
no single central law-school authority like NAAB/AAMC issues this guidance.) [[RULE-COUNSEL-041]]
**This is the single most important unsafe-inference guard for this family**: ORYN must never
imply a student needs to pick a "pre-law" major or a narrow set of "law-track" courses — doing so
would contradict the field's own stated norms and could actively narrow a student's exploration
unnecessarily. [[RULE-COUNSEL-042]]

## What evidence actually differentiates interest from engagement

Named activities: mock trial, moot court, speech and debate, undergraduate/school law journals or
review clubs, a pre-law society with real programming. These build "the same skills tested on the
LSAT and in law school exams" per advising sources — i.e., the evidence value is in the
*transferable skill* (argumentation, written analysis under structure), not in the activity's
surface-level "law" branding. A strong debate or Model UN record (see
`12-political-science-ir-public-policy.md`) is legitimate evidence for this family too — the two
families overlap heavily in evidence type, differing mainly in stated career direction, which
should be read from the student's own goals, not inferred from activity type alone.

## Mapping onto the shipped taxonomy

- `academics` / `intellectual_curiosity` — writing- and argumentation-heavy coursework, broad
  humanities/social-science engagement (deliberately not narrowed to one "correct" major, per §1).
- `execution_project_depth` — mock trial/moot court performance, sustained written work (a school
  law journal contribution, a substantive research paper).
- `career_exploration` — legal internships/shadowing, again access-gated similarly to medicine
  (most direct legal work requires being a law student or paralegal-track adult); realistic
  high-school-stage evidence is shadowing, court observation (many courts allow public observation
  of proceedings, a genuinely low-cost access point worth naming), or volunteer work with a legal-
  aid-adjacent organization.

## Unsafe inferences specific to this family

- **Do not recommend a specific major as "the law path."** (§1, the central finding.)
- **Do not treat a debate/mock-trial trophy as proof of legal aptitude** — it's evidence of
  argumentation skill under a specific competitive format, real but narrower than "being good at
  law," the same caution as MUN in the political-science family.
- **Do not assume legal internships are broadly accessible** — most are effectively gated to
  college-age/law-student applicants; court observation (public, free, low-barrier in most
  jurisdictions) is a comparatively accessible alternative worth surfacing specifically because it
  doesn't share the same access constraint. [[RULE-COUNSEL-043]]

## Country notes (UK / Germany / EU / Turkey)

Career-outlook context throughout, not admissions-evidence guidance — see
`10-medicine-clinical-pathways.md` for the full method/bounding statement.

**United Kingdom:** National Careers Service publishes official profiles for both **Solicitor**
and **Barrister** — the UK's own split legal profession (solicitors advise and act on clients'
behalf; barristers represent clients in court and give specialist advice), a structural difference
from the unified US "lawyer" framing this document's §1 finding (no required pre-law major) still
applies to equally, but worth naming explicitly for a UK-facing student since the *qualification
pathway* differs by which branch they're aiming for. (Source: `nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/
job-profiles/solicitor` and `/barrister` — official, high confidence.) [[RULE-COUNSEL-089]]

**Germany, added on a further pass**: the most exam-intensive model this package has found for any
family. Law study culminates in the **Erste Staatsexamen** (First State Examination), followed by
a mandatory **2-year Referendariat** (legal traineeship rotating across civil court, criminal
court/prosecution, an administrative authority, and a law firm), then a **Zweite Staatsexamen**
(Second State Examination) — only after both exams is a graduate a fully qualified **Volljurist**,
eligible to practice as **Rechtsanwalt** (attorney), judge, prosecutor, or in senior civil service.
Total time from starting law study to bar admission is commonly cited as **7-11 years** — notably
longer than the UK's or US's pathways. (Source: German legal-training guidance sources, including
the Bundesrechtsanwaltskammer/Federal Bar's own published training-structure description — medium-
high confidence for the two-exam/Referendariat structure, which is consistently and specifically
described across sources.) [[RULE-COUNSEL-104]] **This directly sharpens `RULE-COUNSEL-041`'s "no
required pre-law major" finding for a German-track student specifically**: while true that no
single *undergraduate* major is mandated the way, say, medicine requires pre-med coursework, German
legal training itself *is* a single, unified, state-examined pathway with no real equivalent to
the US's flexible "major now, law school later" structure — a German-track student's actual
decision point is different in kind from a US-track one's, not just longer.

**EU:** ESCO covers legal occupations across EU languages (see `10`'s EU note for ESCO's full
description). Legal practice rights are a per-country regulatory/bar-admission matter, not
harmonized EU-wide — Germany's Referendariat-based model and the UK's solicitor/barrister model are
both real, both official, and not interchangeable.

**Turkey:** İŞKUR's official "Geleceğin Meslekleri" list includes **Avukat** (Lawyer) among its 93
designated future occupations. [[RULE-COUNSEL-085]]

## Rules established in this document

- `RULE-COUNSEL-041` — There is no required or specifically-rewarded "pre-law" major; do not imply
  one exists. Confidence: high (consistent, non-controversial consensus across advising sources).
- `RULE-COUNSEL-042` — Recommend broad skill-building (writing, argumentation, critical reasoning)
  across multiple possible majors rather than narrowing a law-interested student's academic path.
  Confidence: high.
- `RULE-COUNSEL-043` — Surface court observation as a comparatively low-access-barrier evidence
  channel for this family, distinct from internships which are more consistently access-gated.
  Confidence: medium (reasoned; public-court-access rules vary by jurisdiction and this package did
  not verify them country-by-country).
- `RULE-COUNSEL-085` — İŞKUR's official Future Occupations list includes Avukat (Lawyer),
  confirming domestic Turkish labor-market relevance for career-exploration framing only.
  Confidence: high (official source, for the list's content).
- `RULE-COUNSEL-089` — For a UK-facing law-interested student, name the solicitor/barrister split
  explicitly since qualification pathways differ by branch, sourced from NCS official profiles.
  Confidence: high (official source).
- `RULE-COUNSEL-104` — For a German-track law student, the "no required pre-law major" finding
  needs sharpening: German legal training itself is a single, unified, two-Staatsexamen-plus-
  Referendariat pathway with no real equivalent to the US's flexible major-then-law-school
  structure. Confidence: medium-high (consistently described across sources, not fetched from a
  single primary German-government page).
