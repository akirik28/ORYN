# 06.03 — Physical Sciences

Covers the mission's "physics" and "chemistry" raw fields. Onboarding anchor: **Physics** (no
dedicated onboarding suggestion for chemistry — see `00-family-taxonomy.md` §4's gap note).

## 1. Field identity and subfields

Physics ranges from experimental (building apparatus, running measurements) to theoretical
(mathematical modeling of physical law) to applied (engineering-adjacent, e.g. materials/photonics).
Chemistry ranges similarly: experimental/bench chemistry, computational/theoretical chemistry, and
applied chemistry (materials science, chemical engineering-adjacent). **RULE-COUNSEL-040:** a
student's stated interest in "physics" or "chemistry" should be probed for which pole they actually
enjoy — theoretical/mathematical problem-solving vs. hands-on experimental work are different
day-to-day activities and reward different exploration experiences (a proof-heavy theoretical
project vs. a lab-based experimental one), directly analogous to the pure/applied split already
named in `02-mathematics-statistics.md` §1.

## 2. Core skills

Per BLS: physicists and astronomers apply "knowledge of science and math" broadly enough that BLS
explicitly notes graduates at every degree level (bachelor's through doctorate) find this knowledge
"useful for entry into many other occupations" beyond physics/astronomy specifically — an
early, explicit signal that this field's *skills* transfer more broadly than its *named occupation*
does [S-BLS-PHYSICS]. For chemists and materials scientists, BLS specifically names teamwork and,
for more senior roles, leadership/team-motivation skills, since "chemists and materials scientists
typically work on research teams" [S-BLS-CHEM] — a useful corrective to any assumption that bench
science is a solitary pursuit. Beyond BLS: mathematical modeling (physics, more heavily
theoretical/experimental depending on subfield), laboratory technique and safety practice
(chemistry, hands-on), and — for both — careful experimental design and error/uncertainty analysis.

## 3. Career families

Beyond academic research (a real but numerically limited path, as in mathematics): physics
graduates commonly move into engineering-adjacent roles, data science/quantitative finance (the
mathematical/analytical skill transfer BLS itself names), medical physics, and materials science/
photonics industry roles. Chemistry graduates commonly move into pharmaceutical/biotech industry
roles, materials science, environmental science-adjacent testing/regulatory roles (see
`09-environmental-science-sustainability.md`), and chemical engineering-adjacent industry roles
(process chemistry). **Counter-stereotypical example:** a substantial fraction of quantitative
finance ("quant") hires come from physics (and mathematics) degrees specifically, not
finance/economics degrees — a well-documented pattern in that industry, illustrating major≠career
concretely.

## 4. Exploration pathways by stage

**Phase 1:** an accessible hands-on experiment or small build (physics: a simple mechanics/
electronics project; chemistry: a supervised home- or school-lab-safe experiment), general
coursework engagement, science-fair participation at an accessible level.

**Phase 2:** the **U.S. National Chemistry Olympiad (USNCO)**, run by the American Chemical
Society, is a concrete, well-documented, multi-tier pathway (local exam each March → national exam
each April → a study camp → international team selection) [S-ACS-OLYMPIAD] — a strong,
verifiable evidence point specifically for chemistry (distinct from a general "did a science
project" claim). ACS's **Project SEED** is an ACS-sponsored paid summer research program
specifically for economically disadvantaged students [S-ACS-OLYMPIAD] — worth surfacing to
students for whom program cost is a real constraint (directly relevant to the founder spec's
"minor-safe"/accessible-by-default product philosophy, not just an incidental detail). More
generally at this stage: a sustained research project (bench or computational), a physics olympiad
track (national/international), or a college-level online course in a specific subfield to test
genuine interest before committing further.

## 5. Interdisciplinary connections

**Physics/chemistry + engineering** (materials science, applied physics): see
`05-engineering-me-ce-ae-ee.md`. **Chemistry + biology** (biochemistry): see `04-life-sciences.md`.
**Physics + computing** (computational physics, quantum computing): see

## 6. Professional/licensure notes

**No general licensure requirement** for physicist or chemist roles as such. Narrow exceptions
exist in specific regulated industries (e.g., certain environmental-testing or pharmaceutical
quality roles operate under regulatory certification regimes for the *lab/facility*, not a personal
license for the scientist) — not elaborated further here as a general caution for pre-university
counseling, since it affects a specific employer/industry context rather than the field of study
itself.

## 7. Country notes (UK / EU / Turkey)

**United Kingdom:** National Careers Service publishes official profiles for chemist, biochemist,
and physicist roles specifically — noting, for chemists, a **Chartered Chemist (CChem)** and
**Chartered Scientist (CSci)** progression path available to experienced practitioners [S-NCS-SCI],
a UK-specific professional-recognition layer with no direct US equivalent (the US has no
chartered-scientist-style title for these roles — recognition there runs through employer/
institutional reputation and, for some roles, board certifications specific to a narrow subfield,
not a single national chartered title). No licensure gate applies to entering physicist/chemist
roles themselves in the UK, matching §6's US picture — chartered status is a career-long
professional-recognition track, not an entry requirement.

**EU:** ESCO covers physical-science occupations across EU languages [S-ESCO — see
`01-computing-information-sciences.md` §7]. No EU-wide licensure gate applies to this family.

**Turkey:** İŞKUR's official "Geleceğin Meslekleri" future-occupations list names **Kimyager**
(Chemist) explicitly [S-ISKUR-FUTURE — see `01-computing-information-sciences.md` §7 for this
source]; physics does not appear as a named standalone entry on that particular list (a list of
93 selected occupations, not exhaustive — its absence is not evidence physics careers aren't
recognized in Turkey, just that this specific document doesn't name one). No detailed official
skills/salary profile source found for either occupation in this pass. Flagged in
`10-open-questions.md`.
