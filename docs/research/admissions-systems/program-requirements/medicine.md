# Medicine — cross-country program-level requirements

Part of ORYN's R3.1 country-level admissions research package, layered on top of (not a
replacement for) each country's own admissions-architecture document in
[`../`](../). See [`../README.md`](../README.md) for the full ruleset (RULE-ADMISSIONS-001
through 017) and package context. Machine-readable version of everything below:
[`../../../../data/research/admissions-systems/program-requirements/medicine.json`](../../../../data/research/admissions-systems/program-requirements/medicine.json).
This document does not re-derive each country's general admissions architecture — it asks a
narrower question on top of it: for Medicine *specifically*, what changes, and is the change a
genuinely different decision mechanism or just a higher bar within the mechanism that country
already uses?

## Overview

Two axes matter for every country in this package, and they are not the same axis.
**Eligibility mechanics** ask what a student must hold or submit to be considered at all.
**Structural mechanism** asks what *kind* of process decides who actually gets a seat — a
threshold check, a national quota allocation, a ranked competition, a two-stage
gateway-then-filter model, a graduate-only category. For most restricted programme families in
this package, "restricted" has meant a higher bar inside the same mechanism the country already
uses elsewhere. Medicine breaks that pattern more often than it confirms it. Across the 15
countries researched, Medicine runs through a **genuinely different decision architecture** from
that same country's general admissions model in 13 of 15 cases — a dedicated national quota
system found nowhere else in the country (Germany); a mandatory aptitude test plus interview
stack absent from general admission (United Kingdom, Ireland, Switzerland, Australia, New
Zealand, Singapore, and Hong Kong's non-JUPAS track); a two-stage entry-then-competitive-filter
model unlike the country's single-stage norm (France, New Zealand); a mechanism that itself
bifurcates by instruction language or linguistic region within one country (Italy, Switzerland);
or, most sharply, a total category mismatch where Medicine is not even an undergraduate
admissions object to begin with (United States, Canada). Only **Turkey** and **Spain's public
track** show Medicine running through literally the same mechanism as general admission, with
an extreme emergent cutoff and nothing else added — the "numerically stricter only" pattern this
research brief anticipated finding commonly. It turned out to be the **minority** pattern, not
the default, and the two clean examples of it are informative precisely because they are rare in
this package.

A second, compounding finding sharpens the first: in countries whose *general* admissions model
is already competitive/ranking-based rather than threshold-based (Australia, Singapore, New
Zealand to a degree), "Medicine is structurally different" cannot simply mean "more
competitive" — that would be true of nearly every popular Australian or Singaporean programme.
The real test in those countries is whether Medicine introduces a *specific added mechanism*
(a dedicated aptitude test, a mandatory interview, a fixed government place-count divorced from
the general funding rule, a separate gateway year) that general admission does not have at all —
not whether Medicine's cutoff is numerically higher than other programmes', which is true almost
everywhere and proves nothing about structure on its own.

## Country-by-country findings

### Germany — structurally different

Direct-entry German programmes run on a largely automatic Abiturnote/HZB-equivalence threshold.
Medicine is one of exactly four subjects (with Dentistry, Veterinary Medicine, Pharmacy) pulled
entirely out of that model into hochschulstart's national NC (numerus clausus) allocation
system — this alone is a different architecture, not a stricter version of the general one.
After a Vorabquote (~20%) is set aside for special applicant groups, remaining seats split three
ways: **Abiturbestenquote** (~30% of the remainder, grade-only ranking), **AdH/Auswahlverfahren
der Hochschulen** — confirmed directly on hochschulstart.de as **60%** of the remainder — and
**Zusätzliche Eignungsquote/ZEQ** (~10%, a non-grade path for weaker-Abitur applicants).

The specific lead this research pass was asked to verify — whether AdH's health-related-service-
experience exception is narrower than commonly assumed — resolves cleanly to **yes, narrower**.
hochschulstart's own AdH rules require universities to use **at least two** school-grade-
independent criteria for Medicine specifically (at least one for Veterinary/Dental Medicine):
options include subject-aptitude test results (TMS/HAM-Nat), "practical activities," or other
relevant achievements. Each university chooses its own combination and weighting from that
list — health-related vocational training, a completed nursing/paramedic apprenticeship, or
FSJ/Bundesfreiwilligendienst service is **one optional criterion a university may pick**, not a
guaranteed alternative admissions path, not present at every medical faculty, and confined
entirely to AdH — it does not exist within Abiturbestenquote or ZEQ at all. Put in seats: at
most, the exception touches a slice of the ~60%-of-post-Vorabquote AdH pool, and only at
whichever specific universities choose to weight it, which is not all of them (reportedly
weighted heavily at Hannover per secondary sources; not confirmed as present at every faculty).
"Germany rewards healthcare volunteering for Medicine applicants" is a real finding compressed
into a false generalization; the accurate version needs the quota, the ≥2-criteria rule, and the
per-university optionality stated together.

### United Kingdom — structurally different

General UK admission runs on predicted-then-confirmed A-Level/IB grades against a UCAS
conditional offer, one shared personal statement, one shared school reference, and rarely an
interview outside Oxbridge. Medicine (with Dentistry/Vet Med/Vet Science) adds three things
general admission has none of. First, a mandatory national aptitude test: **UCAT**, sat in a
fixed summer window (13 Jul–24 Sep 2026 for 2027 entry) well before the October UCAS deadline.
**BMAT is discontinued** — its final sitting was October 2023, and there is no BMAT in 2025 or
2026; former BMAT schools (Oxford, Cambridge, Imperial, UCL, Brighton and Sussex, Lancaster,
Leeds) now all use UCAT instead, so with very few exceptions every UK medical school requires
UCAT for current-cycle entry. Second, a structured interview, commonly **Multiple Mini
Interview (MMI)** format at most schools — a circuit of short stations each assessing one
attribute, distinct from a single conversational interview. Third, a hard, centrally
state-funded national place cap (**Medical and Dental Maximum Fundable Limits**, set by the
Office for Students) because this training is itself state-funded (~£250,000–270,000 per
medical student over 5 years) — a university literally cannot admit beyond its allocated limit
regardless of applicant quality, a genuinely different constraint from an ordinary competitive
course's floating, demand-driven cutoff.

### Netherlands — structurally different, and the mechanism itself varies within the field

General Dutch Bachelor's admission is eligibility-threshold-based: VWO-equivalence plus subject
prerequisites equals admission, no ranking. Medicine falls under the Netherlands' 2023 numerus
fixus legal framework, which lets each institution choose among qualitative selection, lottery
(weighted or unweighted), or a hybrid. The lead this pass verified — which method(s) currently
apply to Medicine specifically, and where — resolves to genuine, confirmed, university-by-
university variation **within the same 2026–27 cycle, for the identical field**: Groningen,
the University of Amsterdam (UvA), and Radboud select **all** Medicine places by **unweighted
lottery**; VU Amsterdam (portfolio plus test) and Leiden use **decentralized qualitative
selection**; Utrecht runs a **hybrid** — roughly 20% decentralized selection and 80% unweighted
lottery for its 297 places in 2026–27. This means "the Netherlands uses lottery for Medicine"
and "the Netherlands uses selection for Medicine" are both wrong as country-level claims, even
restricted to this one field and this one cycle — the correct unit of description is the
individual university. Registration strategy also differs for Medicine specifically: capped at
just **1** numerus fixus registration (vs. 2 for other numerus fixus fields), with an earlier
**15 January** deadline rather than the general 1 May date.

### Italy — structurally different, and bifurcated by instruction language

Italian-taught Medicine and Surgery (with Dentistry, Veterinary Medicine) was reformed starting
a.y. 2025/26: applicants get **free, open, no-test enrollment** into a first "**semestre
filtro**" (filter semester) — the secondary diploma is the only requirement — and selection
instead happens via rank order on three propaedeutic exams (Chemistry & Biochemical
Propaedeutics, Physics, Biology; 6 CFU each) sat *during* that semester itself (current
governing rule for a.y. 2026/27: MUR Decreto Ministeriale n. 941 of 10 July 2026). **English-
taught Medicine and Surgery is explicitly not covered by this reform** and continues to use
**IMAT**, an up-front, competitive, MUR-owned/CINECA-administered national entrance test sat
before enrollment even begins. This is a genuine within-country, within-field bifurcation by
instruction language, not a general-vs-selective split — both tracks are already "numero
chiuso"; they resolve selectivity at structurally different points in time (during the first
semester vs. before enrollment) using different instruments entirely. The old name "TOLC-MED"
is obsolete for the Italian-taught track as of a.y. 2025/26, though some university pages
(including Sapienza's own page title) still reference it.

### Canada — structurally different, in the sharpest sense: Medicine is not an undergraduate admissions object

Canadian undergraduate admission is generally a grades-threshold/admission-average model
evaluated per faculty. Medicine sits entirely outside that model, and the country's own doc in
this package left this thin — a targeted follow-up this pass confirms Canada shares the United
States' structural pattern. Every Canadian MD programme reviewed requires a prior,
substantially completed undergraduate degree before a candidate can even apply. University of
Toronto's own admissions page states directly that a candidate "cannot enter a program directly
from high school" and must complete at least 3 years (15 full course equivalents) of a
bachelor's degree first; McMaster requires 3 years of prior full-time undergraduate study in any
discipline. Applications route through OMSAS (Ontario) or each school's own graduate-entry
process, evaluated on undergraduate GPA, MCAT (required at most schools, though not at Quebec's
medical schools, University of Ottawa, or the Northern Ontario School of Medicine), references,
and commonly a CASPer-style situational-judgement test — an entirely different evidence stack
from any *undergraduate* Canadian admissions model in this package. Unlike the United States,
**no BS/MD-style direct-from-high-school combined pathway was found in sources reviewed for
Canada** — read as a genuine gap, not a confirmed absence, but a real asymmetry worth flagging:
the two North American graduate-entry systems in this package are not identical in every
respect.

### Switzerland — structurally different, and bifurcated by linguistic region

Swiss Bachelor's admission for Matura holders is otherwise essentially automatic and threshold-
based. Medicine (with Dentistry, Veterinary Medicine, Chiropractic) breaks that pattern, and
breaks it **two different ways depending on linguistic region** — confirmed independently this
pass via swissuniversities' own admissions page. Basel, Bern, Fribourg, Zurich (plus its
Lucerne/St. Gallen joint-Medicine tracks), USI, and ETH Zurich all use **EMS** (Eignungstest für
das Medizinstudium), a full-day pre-entry aptitude test that activates once applications exceed
120% of available places (i.e., demand exceeds supply by more than 20%, matching the existing
country doc's phrasing exactly from an independent source this pass). **Geneva, Lausanne, and
Neuchâtel — the French-speaking universities — do not use EMS at all**; they admit broadly into
first-year Medicine and apply a competitive first-year/propaedeutic exam to determine who
progresses into year two. Same restricted field, same country, two genuinely different gate
mechanisms — pre-entry test vs. post-entry first-year filter — split by linguistic region rather
than by individual university preference. Treating EMS as "how Switzerland selects Medicine
students" would misdirect any Geneva/Lausanne/Neuchâtel-track applicant entirely.

### France — structurally different: a two-stage entry-then-filter model

Ordinary non-selective Licence entry in France is a largely threshold/right-based model once the
Bac is held; "licences en tension" add only a soft attendus-fit ranking layer when demand
exceeds capacity. Medicine (grouped with Pharmacy, Dentistry, Midwifery, Physiotherapy as
"**MMOPK**") runs an entirely different two-stage model. First-year **PASS or LAS** entry
happens through ordinary Parcoursup registration, open to any Bac holder — genuinely
open-access at this stage, unlike everything else about Medicine elsewhere in this package. The
actual restriction bites only at **second-year progression**, a separate, internal, competitive
process run by each university (two "groupes d'épreuves" — continuous assessment plus written
exams, then oral exams for the mid-ranking band), with final progression determined by rank
order against a per-university capacity figure set jointly with the Regional Health Agency
(ARS) — reflecting local clinical/training capacity, not a national quota. A further reform is
reportedly planned for 2027; its mechanics were not available in sources reviewed this pass and
should be treated as forward-looking and unconfirmed.

### Spain — numerically stricter only (public track), with a structurally different private-sector caveat

Spain's public Medicine admission runs through **literally the same uniform** nota de acceso/
nota de admisión formula and regional Distrito Único ranked-allocation mechanism as every other
public Grado programme — there is no separate national test, quota system, or interview layer
for public Medicine. What makes it "restricted" is purely that its nota de corte (the
self-adjusting, demand-driven cutoff — the lowest admitted student's score that cycle) lands
extremely high, illustratively around 11.4–13.4 out of a possible 14 at several public
universities in one recent cycle. This is the same mechanism as every other Spanish public
degree, just with an extreme emergent cutoff — one of only two genuinely clean "numerically
stricter only" cases found in this entire package. The caveat matters, though: private Spanish
universities commonly run Medicine through an entirely separate admissions process (own written
science tests, an aptitude test, a personal interview) completely outside the nota de corte/
Distrito Único system — a structurally different pathway, but one that reflects Spain's general
public/private admissions split rather than something Medicine-specific; the same public/private
divergence would apply to any oversubscribed private-university field, not uniquely to Medicine.

### Ireland — structurally different: the one field nationally with a second scored instrument

Virtually every other CAO/points route course in Ireland is decided by Leaving Certificate
points alone — the best 6 subject grades plus any Maths bonus, no other instrument involved.
Medicine is the one field nationally where a second, entirely separate instrument —
**HPAT-Ireland** (Health Professions Admission Test: logical reasoning, interpersonal
understanding, non-verbal reasoning; sat around February/March, before Leaving Cert results) —
combines with LC points into a moderated total, uniformly at all 6 Irish medical schools
(University of Galway, University of Limerick, TCD, UCC, UCD, RCSI). The lead this pass verified
— the 2027-entry weighting change flagged as unconfirmed by a peer research lane — is now
**confirmed directly**: through 2026 entry, the maximum combined score is **865** (LC moderated
to a 565 cap above 550 points, HPAT scored to 300); from **2027 entry**, LC points above 550 are
no longer moderated (full points to a 625 maximum apply) and HPAT is capped at **150** — a new
combined maximum of **775**. This is an officially announced, dated rebalancing toward LC
results and away from HPAT, driven by the Irish Universities Association's own stated finding
that LC performance predicts course completion better than HPAT does. Any cited HPAT/LC
weighting must be flagged with which entry year it applies to — the two figures are materially
different. Separately, non-EU-route Medicine (e.g., UCD's own non-EU Medicine table) runs an
entirely different, **HPAT-free, interview-based** mechanism for the identical named degree —
UCD's non-EU Medicine criteria table makes no mention of HPAT or Leaving Certificate points at
all.

### Australia — structurally different, against an already-competitive baseline

Australia's general undergraduate model is already a capacity-constrained, ranked-allocation
system for virtually all programmes — published ATAR/selection-rank cutoffs are retrospective
outcomes of that cycle's demand, not fixed thresholds set in advance. So "Medicine is more
competitive" alone understates what's actually different, which is two specific additions.
First: Commonwealth Supported Place (CSP) funding for the large majority of Australian degrees
is allocated to each university **in dollars**, with the university deciding its own
course-level place mix; Medicine is the Australian government's own confirmed, explicit
exception (verified directly via studyassist.gov.au this pass), where "the government allocates
a set number of CSPs to each university" — a categorically different funding mechanism, not
merely a smaller number of the same kind of allocation. Second: on top of that fixed place
ceiling, entry additionally requires **UCAT ANZ** (most universities in the consortium) or
**ISAT** (a separate test specifically for defined international-applicant categories at a
subset of the same universities) plus a structured interview (e.g., Monash's MMI format) —
neither of which exists in Australia's general ATAR-only pathway.

### New Zealand — structurally different: a two-stage gateway-then-progression model

Most named NZ Bachelor's programmes use a single-stage, graduated Rank Score threshold (a
continuous NCEA-credit-based score) directly at the point of school-leaver admission. Medicine
breaks this into **two structurally separate stages**, confirmed independently at both
providers researched: entry into Health Sciences First Year (Otago) or an equivalent first-year
gateway (Auckland) via the ordinary University Entrance/Rank Score pathway, followed by a
**separate, competitive progression decision** into second-year Medicine itself, based on a
best-seven-papers grade average plus UCAT ANZ (sat once yearly, 1 July–5 August) — and, at
Auckland specifically, a Multiple Mini Interview for shortlisted candidates. The two providers
also weight UCAT differently: Auckland treats it as roughly 15% of final selection alongside the
MMI; Otago applies it more as a minimum threshold than a heavily-ranked factor. Gateway-year
entry should never be read as equivalent to admission into Medicine itself — it is an earlier,
easier step than the actual second-year progression decision.

### Hong Kong — structurally different within the pathway that matters for ORYN's population

JUPAS is categorically closed to non-local applicants regardless of field (RULE-ADMISSIONS-013),
so a Turkish or other internationally-educated student targeting HK Medicine is, by definition,
in the **non-JUPAS** pathway — the same pathway used for every other non-JUPAS programme. Within
non-JUPAS, most programmes are evaluated by transcript/qualification review against a published
minimum, without an interview. Medicine specifically adds a structured interview most other
non-JUPAS programmes don't require: **HKU** uses a Multiple Mini Interview (MMI) format
(interviewers may switch to Chinese to test proficiency); **CUHK** uses a panel format. CUHK
Medicine also carries an explicit hard place ceiling (300 HKDSE-track plus 25 advanced-standing
places from 2025 entry) and describes non-JUPAS Medicine applicants as evaluated against roughly
a "top 1–2% HKDSE-equivalent" standard — a categorically higher bar than most other non-JUPAS
programmes' published minimums, not merely a slightly higher one. Within the *local* JUPAS
track itself (not the population most Turkish/international applicants sit in), Medicine
instead looks more like the "numerically stricter" pattern — same merit-order matching
algorithm as every other JUPAS programme, just a far higher effective bar — a useful reminder
that a country's Medicine mechanism can be structurally different in one applicant population
and merely stricter in another, simultaneously.

### Singapore — structurally different, against an already-competitive baseline

Singapore's general Autonomous University admission model is already competitive/ranking-based
for virtually every programme — NUS's own language states admission is by "open competition
among all eligible applicants," not threshold-based the way the Netherlands'/Italy's
non-selective majority is. So Medicine's marginal structural difference isn't "competitive vs.
eligible-equals-admitted" — it is two specific additions the general model doesn't have. First,
a **ranking-position gate**: Medicine (and Dentistry, Law) must be ranked within a candidate's
first one or two choices on the application form to even be considered for shortlisting, a rule
most programmes don't carry. Second, a **mandatory standardized-test layer** most NUS programmes
don't require at all: IB-track Medicine/Dentistry applicants submit predicted results plus
**either** UCAT (UK or ANZ) **or** [SAT/ACT-with-Writing **and** AP Exams], on top of whatever
additional interview/test the university runs for the programme. This mechanism was documented
to depth at NUS specifically; broadly analogous restrictions likely exist at NTU and SMU but
were not independently confirmed to the same depth this pass.

### Turkey — numerically stricter only, confirmed

Confirmed directly, consistent with the sibling research lane's finding: Turkish Medicine
carries **no special admission mechanism beyond YKS**. Placement is determined completely by the
same (TYT × 0.40) + (AYT × 0.60) + (OBP × 0.12 or 0.06) formula, compared in strict national
rank order against other candidates who listed the same programme code, using the SAY score
type — identical in kind to every other lisans programme nationally. What makes Medicine
"restricted" is purely that it requires a score close to the maximum attainable, alongside Law
and top-university Engineering — explicitly stated in this package's own Turkey research:
"Medicine, Law, top Engineering programmes remain pure score-ranked placement with no interview
or portfolio step at all." Contrasted against Germany's national-quota system, the UK's
UCAT-plus-MMI-plus-government-cap stack, Ireland's dual-instrument formula, or the Netherlands'
university-varying selection methods, Turkey's Medicine is the single cleanest illustration in
this package of "numerically stricter, not structurally different" — the same mechanism, an
extreme cutoff, and nothing else.

### United States — structurally different, in the sharpest sense: Medicine is not an undergraduate admissions object

There is no "undergraduate Medicine admissions mechanism" to compare against the rest of the US
system the way there is for the Netherlands, Germany, UK, or Ireland — asking "what does US
Medicine require of a high schooler" is close to a category error for the dominant pattern.
Medicine (MD/DO) in the US is, overwhelmingly, a purely **post-baccalaureate graduate
programme**: applicants apply via AMCAS (or AACOMAS for DO) after completing, or nearly
completing, a bachelor's degree, submitting undergraduate GPA, MCAT score, letters of
evaluation, and clinical/research/service experience accumulated during college — an entirely
separate, later application event from undergraduate admission, evaluated by medical schools
rather than by the undergraduate institution at all. "Pre-med" describes prerequisite coursework
and experience completed during any undergraduate major, not a distinct admissions category or
major itself — confirmed via AAMC's own materials, which state the only universal requirement
across every US medical school is a completed bachelor's degree, with no required major (roughly
60% of MCAT examinees majored in a biological science, by AAMC's own data, but this is a
pattern, not a rule). The one real, named, and directly AAMC-documented exception: roughly **40
combined BS/MD (or BA/MD) programmes** nationally (examples include Brown's PLME, Northwestern's
HPME, UMKC's 6-year BA/MD, Rice/Baylor, Case Western, Boston University) admit a small number of
students directly from high school into a linked undergraduate-then-MD pathway — a genuine
direct-entry route that a counselor should still surface for a strongly-qualified 17-year-old,
but a minority, individually-designed, highly selective exception to the dominant graduate-entry
pattern, not the standard route a US-track ORYN user should expect.

## What determines eligibility

Eligibility is never a single fact for Medicine the way it can be for an open-access programme
elsewhere in this package — it stacks. At minimum: (1) the underlying secondary qualification
meeting that country's general higher-education entry bar (exactly as for any other programme);
(2) wherever a dedicated instrument exists (UCAT/UCAT ANZ in the UK/Ireland-adjacent/Australia/
NZ/Singapore cluster, HPAT in Ireland, EMS in most of Switzerland, TMS/HAM-Nat as an optional
German AdH criterion, IMAT for English-taught Italian Medicine), a qualifying score on that
instrument, sat on its own fixed calendar that commonly falls *before* the general application
deadline; (3) wherever an interview exists (UK, most of Australia, Auckland, HKU/CUHK non-JUPAS,
NUS), passing it as a distinct pass-gate, not a scored addend folded into the same number; and
(4) in the two graduate-entry systems (US, Canada), an entirely separate, later eligibility
event — a completed or near-completed bachelor's degree — that supersedes anything evaluable
from secondary school. Nowhere in this package does eligibility for Medicine collapse to "meets
the same bar as any other programme" except Turkey and Spain's public track.

## What genuinely strengthens an application

Where a dedicated aptitude test exists, preparation for and performance on that specific
instrument is the highest-leverage lever found in this research — UCAT/UCAT ANZ score bands
recur across the UK, Ireland-adjacent systems, Australia, New Zealand, and Singapore's IB-track
Medicine pathway as a genuinely differentiating factor in a way no other tested subject in this
package is used this broadly. Where an interview exists, demonstrated interview/MMI performance
matters as its own distinct gate, not folded into academic score. Health-related, subject-
adjacent experience (clinical shadowing, hospital/care volunteering, relevant vocational
training) strengthens an application only where a named mechanism exists to receive it — Germany's
optional AdH criterion (university-chosen, one of several options), the UK's "super-curricular"
personal-statement framing, Singapore's structured achievements list — and does essentially
nothing in systems with no such channel (Turkey, Spain's public track, France's PASS/LAS
first-year stage, the domestic CAO points formula in Ireland outside HPAT itself). For the two
graduate-entry systems, the entire lever set changes: undergraduate GPA trajectory, MCAT score,
and clinical/research experience accumulated *during* university are what matters — nothing
achievable in secondary school moves this needle under the dominant pattern, with the narrow
exception of the US's named BS/MD programmes.

## What is largely irrelevant

Personal statements and recommendation letters are largely or entirely absent as Medicine-
specific levers in the Netherlands (recommendation letters), Germany, Italy, Spain's public
track, France's PASS/LAS filter stage, and Turkey's domestic pathway — consistent with each of
those countries' general-admission pattern already found elsewhere in this package, not a
Medicine-specific finding. General extracurricular breadth unconnected to healthcare or the
specific tested/interviewed competencies is not a meaningful lever anywhere Medicine was
researched this pass — even in systems that do have a structured achievements channel
(Singapore's NUS list, the UK's personal statement), what counts is subject-adjacent depth, not
breadth. In the two graduate-entry systems, a secondary-school student's current activities and
grades are simply the wrong evidence category entirely for the dominant Medicine pathway — they
matter for undergraduate admission in general, not for "Medicine" as a target.

## What ORYN must never infer

Never present any single country's Medicine mechanism as representative of "how Medicine
admission works" generally — the 15-country spread in this document is itself the evidence
against a universal model. Never infer that a health-related-experience or volunteering
credential functions as a guaranteed Medicine-admissions lever anywhere without first checking
whether that country has a *named mechanism* to receive it (confirmed for a subset of German
AdH criteria only, among the countries researched) — assuming it transfers by analogy to Turkey,
Spain's public track, or France's PASS/LAS stage would be actively wrong. Never assume a test
name (UCAT, IMAT, EMS, HPAT, TMS) or format (MMI vs. panel) generalizes across the universities
that use it, or across countries that share a similarly-named consortium test (UCAT UK and UCAT
ANZ are related but administered to different consortia). Never assume BMAT is still current —
it is discontinued. Never assume a percentage or point-cap figure (Germany's AdH quota, Ireland's
HPAT weighting, Spain's illustrative nota de corte) is stable across cycles without checking
which academic cycle it was reported for — Ireland's own HPAT weighting is a confirmed, dated
example of this changing materially between cycles. Never default a US- or Canada-track student
with a stated Medicine interest into the same "which restricted programme to target" framing
used for every other country in this package — the correct question there is which undergraduate
path and coursework sets up a strong post-bachelor's application, a categorically different
counseling problem.

## Institution-specific vs. programme-specific vs. country-wide patterns

**Country-wide, uniform**: Germany's three-quota NC structure (though AdH criteria choice is
university-level within it); Ireland's HPAT-plus-points formula (uniform across all 6 medical
schools, unlike almost every other restricted field in this package, which typically varies by
institution); Turkey's YKS-only mechanism; the structural fact (not the specific numbers) that
US and Canadian Medicine are graduate-entry-only.

**Programme/track-specific within one country**: Italy's Italian-taught-vs-English-taught split
(semestre filtro vs. IMAT); Switzerland's EMS-region-vs-French-region split; France's PASS/LAS
first-year-vs-second-year split; Hong Kong's JUPAS-vs-non-JUPAS split (which determines Medicine's
character entirely, before Medicine-specific facts even apply); Singapore's ranking-position-gate
rule (Medicine, Dentistry, Law share it; most NUS programmes do not).

**Genuinely institution-specific, varying even within one field in one country**: the Netherlands'
Medicine selection method (lottery at three universities, qualitative selection at two, a hybrid
at a sixth, confirmed for the identical 2026–27 cycle); UK Medicine's specific UCAT cutoff and
interview format (MMI vs. panel) per school; Australia's UCAT-ANZ-vs-ISAT applicant-type split,
which varies by university; New Zealand's UCAT weighting (roughly 15% plus MMI at Auckland,
threshold-only at Otago); Hong Kong's HKU-MMI-vs-CUHK-panel split and differing published minimum
thresholds for the same qualification type.

The practical implication for ORYN: a "Medicine requirement" data model needs at minimum a
country-level mechanism-type field, a programme/track-level override (language of instruction,
linguistic region, local/non-local applicant type), and an institution-level override for the
specific instrument, weighting, and interview format — collapsing any of these three levels into
one record would misrepresent at least half the countries in this package.

## Unresolved questions

Germany: the exact current AdH point-weighting for health-related-experience criteria
specifically — which of the roughly 40 medical faculties actually select it as one of their
required school-grade-independent criteria — was not exhaustively confirmed university-by-
university this pass; the 60% AdH quota size and the ≥2-criteria rule are confirmed at
hochschulstart's own national level, the per-university criterion choice is not exhaustively
mapped. Netherlands: the full national distribution of which selection method every one of the
roughly 8 Medicine faculties uses for 2026–27 was sampled (6 of ~8), not exhaustively confirmed
for every faculty — Erasmus MC's and Maastricht's exact current split were not conclusively
pinned down this pass. Canada: whether any BS/MD-equivalent direct-from-high-school combined
pathway exists anywhere in Canada was not found in sources reviewed — a genuine gap, not a
