# Economics / Business — cross-country program-level requirements

Part of ORYN's R3.1 country-level admissions research package, layered on top of (not a
replacement for) each country's own admissions-architecture document in
[`../`](../). See [`../README.md`](../README.md) for the full ruleset (RULE-ADMISSIONS-001
through 017) and package context. Machine-readable version of everything below:
[`../../../../data/research/admissions-systems/program-requirements/economics-business.json`](../../../../data/research/admissions-systems/program-requirements/economics-business.json).
This document does not re-derive each country's general admissions architecture — it asks a
narrower question on top of it: for Economics/Business *specifically*, what changes, and is
the change a genuinely different decision mechanism or just a higher bar within the mechanism
that country already uses?

## Overview

Economics/Business is a genuinely mixed case, not a lopsided one — the opposite shape from
Medicine, which this package's sibling research found structurally different from the general
admissions model in 13 of the same 15 countries. Across these 15 countries, Economics/Business
splits roughly evenly: **structurally different in 5** (United States, United Kingdom,
Netherlands, Canada, France), **the same underlying mechanism with a higher bar in 4** (Italy,
Germany, Spain, Turkey), and **running through each country's ordinary general-admission
mechanism with no dedicated overlay at all in the remaining 6** (Ireland, Australia, New
Zealand, Switzerland, Singapore, Hong Kong) — several of which required checking specifically
*because* the destination institution (St. Gallen, SMU) is itself an economics/business-focused
institution, which invites conflating "this institution's general admission" with "a
field-specific gate."

The second, compounding finding is about the *shape* of structural difference where it exists.
Medicine's structural differences are almost always about capacity-constrained professional
licensure — a government-funded training-place ceiling, a national quota, a mandatory aptitude
test tied to clinical competence. Economics/Business's structural differences, where found, are
instead almost always about **prestige-tier stratification** layered on top of an otherwise
open or moderately competitive field: a separate, harder-to-reach elite inner track sitting
alongside ordinary access to the same broad subject. The clearest examples: France's prépa
ECG/ECT-to-BCE/Ecricome-concours pathway runs alongside an open, non-selective Licence
Économie-Gestion in the very same country; several elite US universities gate their named
business school as its own freshman-application object while Business/Economics as a general
field of study remains ordinary at most other US universities; the UK's TMUA applies at a
defined cluster of quantitatively-elite departments (Cambridge, LSE, Imperial, UCL) while most
UK Economics programmes require no test at all; the Netherlands numerus-fixus-caps the
English/international track of Economics and Business Economics at UvA while the identical
Dutch-taught track of the *same* programme stays open-admission; Canada's leading Commerce
programmes (Queen's Smith, Rotman, Sauder, Schulich) layer a mandatory, rubric-scored, real-time
video-and-written supplementary application on top of ordinary grades-based review, a genuinely
different evidence-collection mechanism from an activities list. Nowhere in this family does a
government cap the total number of Economics/Business training places the way the UK and
Australia cap Medicine places, and nowhere does Economics/Business require a dedicated national
aptitude test analogous to UCAT or HPAT — the structural differences that do exist are
elite-tier access mechanisms, not field-wide capacity or licensure gates.

## Country-by-country findings

### United States — structurally different, institution-specific

At a defined, non-universal cluster of US universities, undergraduate Business is not a major a
student declares after enrolling — it is a separate school with its own admissions gate,
evaluated apart from (sometimes instead of, sometimes in addition to) the general university
admission most other US applicants experience. This is a genuinely different admissions
*object*, not a stricter version of the general Common App/holistic-review model already
documented for the US, but the specific mechanism varies sharply even among the three named
universities checked this pass. **University of Texas at Austin's McCombs School** runs
direct-admit-to-major at the freshman application itself: applicants list a specific BBA major,
McCombs evaluates them apart from (more selectively than) UT's automatic-admission baseline, at
roughly a 9–10% acceptance rate; students not directly admitted can pursue internal transfer
later via a defined 30-credit-hour Business Foundations coursework track (Calculus I/II,
Micro/Macro, BA 101/102). **Indiana University's Kelley School** announced a genuine, dated
mechanism change effective **fall 2026**: previously a narrower/more metric-driven direct-admit
pool, now every applicant who indicates a Kelley major is considered for direct admission
through a comprehensive holistic academic review (course rigor, especially quantitative
subjects) at the point of the freshman application itself — letters of recommendation were
explicitly dropped from the evaluation. Students not directly admitted may still join Kelley
after one year with defined course/GPA requirements. **Michigan's Ross School** underwent the
sharpest recent shift: through fall 2024, Ross was reachable only via "Preferred Admission" — a
two-step process requiring admission to another Michigan school (Engineering, Kinesiology,
Music/Theatre/Dance, or Stamps Art & Design) first, with transfer into Ross in year two.
Starting **fall 2025**, Ross became a First-Year Admitting Unit on the Common App, meaning
direct freshman admission to Ross now exists alongside continued (narrower) Preferred Admission
from a smaller set of other schools — but this traded away a former safety net: students who
apply directly to Ross and are rejected no longer have the fallback of being admitted to LSA
instead, since Preferred Admission via the College of Engineering route to Ross was eliminated.
All three differ from most US universities, where Business/Economics is an ordinary major
requiring nothing beyond the general application already documented for the US country doc —
none of the three should be treated as representative of how "US direct-admit business school"
works generally.

### United Kingdom — structurally different, programme/institution-specific

General UK admission (already documented) runs on predicted-then-confirmed grades against a
UCAS conditional offer, one shared personal statement, and no test for most subjects. At a
defined cluster of universities, Economics specifically adds a mandatory pre-interview
quantitative admissions test the general model has none of — but which specific test, and
whether it is mandatory or merely reward-bearing, genuinely differs by institution, and the
same-sounding requirement splits into two *different* tests depending on which flagship
university a student targets. **TMUA** (Test of Mathematics for University Admission) is the
central pre-interview test for Cambridge Economics and is mandatory for LSE Economics (2027
entry); Imperial requires it for Economics, Finance and Data Science; UCL requires it
specifically for its Economics BSc (Econ) and Economics with a Year Abroad BSc (Econ) degrees.
Warwick and Durham instead treat a strong TMUA score as reward-bearing (a reduced offer) rather
than mandatory. **Oxford's Economics-adjacent courses — PPE, Economics and Management, History
and Economics — do not use TMUA at all**: for 2027 entry Oxford introduced an entirely new
test, **TARA** (Test of Academic Reasoning for Admissions, replacing the discontinued TSA),
specifically for these courses plus Human Sciences and the Psychology-adjacent courses — a
three-module (Critical Thinking, Problem Solving, Writing Task) computer-based test delivered
via Pearson VUE. A counselor defaulting a Cambridge-Economics-track student's TMUA preparation
onto an Oxford PPE applicant, or vice versa, would prepare the wrong test entirely — these are
not two names for the same instrument.

### Netherlands — structurally different, varying within the same programme by language track

General Dutch Bachelor's admission (already documented) is eligibility-threshold-based: VWO-
equivalence plus subject prerequisites equals admission for open programmes, with numerus
fixus as the exception mechanism. Economics/Business is a genuine, confirmed exception field at
several universities — but the sharpest finding this pass is that the *same* named programme,
at the *same* university, teaching the *same* content, can be open in one language track and
numerus fixus in the other. **UvA's Economics and Business Economics** Bachelor's runs two
tracks: the Dutch track has no selection procedure at all (numerus fixus does not apply), while
the English track is capped at **850** first-year places and requires passing a selection test
assessing Mathematics, Economics, and Principles of Economics and Business — confirmed directly
on UvA's own programme pages. This is a materially different shape from Italy's Medicine
language-bifurcation (documented in this package's Medicine research), where both instruction-
language tracks were still restricted, just via different mechanisms — here, one track is
genuinely open-admission and the other genuinely capped, for identical content. Beyond UvA,
**Erasmus School of Economics'** International Bachelor Economics and Business Economics
(IBEB) is a confirmed numerus fixus programme (**700 places** for 2026–27), using a hybrid
mechanism: the best-ranked applicants (via grades plus a curriculum-based activity/test)
receive the first 350 ranking numbers directly, the remainder are allocated via weighted
lottery. **Tilburg's BSc Economics** is also numerus fixus, ranked by average grade, a
knowledge test, and possibly a motivation letter. **UvA's PPLE** (Politics, Psychology, Law and
Economics) is numerus fixus at roughly 300 places against several thousand applicants, using
grades, a motivation letter, and an online assessment. Economics/Business is therefore one of
the more commonly-capped non-health fields in the Dutch system — a genuine correction to any
assumption that only Medicine-adjacent fields carry numerus fixus.

### Canada — structurally different, institution-specific but confirmed common, not an outlier

General Canadian undergraduate admission (already documented) is predominantly grades/
transcript-based, with essay requirements genuinely varying by university — U of T's general
Arts & Science reviews transcript only, while UBC's Personal Profile is a well-documented,
heavily-weighted exception. Several of Canada's leading Commerce/Business programmes sharpen
this exception into its own distinct pattern: a **mandatory supplementary application built on
a timed, rubric-scored written-plus-video response format** (commonly delivered via the Kira
Talent platform), evaluating durable traits demonstrated in the moment rather than a
retrospective activities inventory. **Queen's Smith School of Business (Commerce)** confirms
this directly: applicants complete a timed written response and a timed video response (2
minutes to prepare, 2 minutes to record, one randomly-drawn question from a small prompt bank),
scored against an official rubric assessing description of challenge, initiative, problem-
solving ability, and reflection — explicitly not a review of an activities list. **This is not
a Queen's-specific outlier**: the identical structural shape recurs at the University of
Toronto's **Rotman Commerce** (a mandatory supplemental application meaningfully weighted
alongside grades), **UBC Sauder's BCom** (a "Personal Profile" combining short written
responses and a video interview), and York's **Schulich BBA** (video interview responses, a
timed written essay, and a leadership profile, explicitly weighted equally with high-school
academics). Western's **Ivey Business School** uses a third, different shape again: a delayed
"Advanced Entry Opportunity" — entered from high school but not formally admitted to Ivey until
year three, via its own competitive process (already documented in the existing Canada country
doc). By contrast, no equivalent supplementary-application requirement was found for **McGill's
Desautels BCom** in sources reviewed this pass — a genuine, lower-confidence gap rather than a
confirmed absence, but a real signal that even among Canada's most prominent Commerce
programmes this model, while common, is not universal.

### France — structurally different: a business-school-specific elite parallel pathway

This resolves the lead directly: France's Grandes Écoles de Commerce are reached through an
elite parallel pathway with the identical dual-stage *shape* as engineering's CPGE-to-concours
system, but running through entirely separate, business-school-specific institutions and
consortia — not a subset or variant of the engineering pathway. Entry into the 2-year "classe
préparatoire économique et commerciale" (**CPGE ECG**, the general track, or **ECT**, the
technological-Bac track) happens exactly like engineering CPGE: via Parcoursup, on academic
dossier only (première/terminale grades, teacher appreciations, a short motivation statement),
evaluated by a commission of the prépa's own teachers with no ranking algorithm — confirmed as
one of Parcoursup's "formations sélectives," the same procedural category as engineering CPGE,
categorically different from ordinary non-selective Licences. The genuine restriction does not
bite at this entry point: it bites two years later, at a separate, high-stakes **"concours"**
run entirely outside Parcoursup by inter-school exam consortia — and there are confirmed to be
(at least) two parallel, non-overlapping business-school consortia, mirroring engineering's own
plurality of concours (Mines-Ponts, Centrale-Supélec, e3a, etc.). The **BCE** (Banque Commune
d'Épreuves), run by the Paris Île-de-France Chamber of Commerce and Industry, gives access to
18 schools including the "trio de tête" HEC Paris, ESSEC, and ESCP, plus EDHEC, emlyon,
Audencia, SKEMA and others — written exams run roughly mid-April to end of May, followed by
oral exams roughly mid-June to early July (2026 cycle). **Ecricome** is a separate,
non-overlapping 5-school consortium (EM Strasbourg, KEDGE, Montpellier Business School, NEOMA,
Rennes School of Business), on its own staggered calendar so a candidate can sit both; Ecricome
added new parallel-entry voies for 2026 (a D1/D2 track for literary-prépa students and a
scientific-prépa track) alongside its core ECG/ECT voie. This entire structure runs alongside,
not instead of, an ordinary, open, Parcoursup-direct **Licence Économie-Gestion** — already
documented in the existing France country doc as one of the named "licences en tension" fields
(nominally non-selective, softly ranked by attendus-fit only when locally oversubscribed under
the 2018 loi ORE) — meaning a French student's path into "Economics/Business" bifurcates as
sharply by *chosen pathway* as an engineering-track student's does, into either an open/soft-
ranked university Licence or a genuinely selective, two-stage prépa-then-concours elite track,
both nominally in the same broad subject area.

### Italy — numerically stricter only

Economics runs through the identical CISIA TOLC test-family mechanism already documented for
the general Italian model, with a dedicated TOLC-Economics variant among the family's
subject-specific tests. Exactly as for the general model: at open Economics programmes, the
test is a non-binding orientation/placement check (a low score can trigger the OFA remedial
module, not a denial); at programmes an individual university chooses to cap under "numero
programmato locale," the identical test becomes the binding, ranked selection criterion. This
is a university-level decision, not a national one — Economics is explicitly named (alongside
Engineering, Architecture, Psychology, Communication) as one of the fields individual
universities commonly choose to cap locally, varying institution-by-institution and
year-by-year, with no nationally uniform list. No dedicated, Economics-specific test, quota, or
interview layer beyond this existing TOLC mechanism was found.

### Germany — numerically stricter only

Business Administration (Betriebswirtschaftslehre/BWL) is one of the most commonly-cited
examples of a subject placed under **local NC** (örtlicher Numerus Clausus) by individual
German universities — but local NC is explicitly documented as a different layer from, and the
same threshold-based Abiturnote mechanism as, the rest of German direct-entry admission, just
with a university-set, self-defined, fluctuating cutoff that responds purely to that specific
institution's demand that cycle. This is categorically different from Medicine's national NC:
hochschulstart's centrally-coordinated quota system is confirmed to cover exactly four subjects
— Human Medicine, Dentistry, Veterinary Medicine, Pharmacy — and Business/Economics is not, and
has never been, one of them. No dedicated German aptitude test analogous to TMS/HAM-Nat, and no
national quota structure, was found for Business/Economics anywhere in sources reviewed.

### Spain — numerically stricter only, with an unconfirmed private-sector caveat

ADE (Administración y Dirección de Empresas) and Economía run through the literally identical
nota de acceso/nota de admisión formula and regional Distrito Único ranked-allocation mechanism
as every other public Grado — no separate national test, quota system, or interview layer was
found, the same clean pattern already confirmed for Spain's public-track Medicine in this
package's Medicine research. What makes top ADE programmes restrictive is purely a high,
demand-driven nota de corte: confirmed for the 2025–26 cycle at roughly **11.9–12.0** (out of
14) at Universidad Complutense de Madrid and roughly **11.0–11.5** at Universidad Carlos III de
Madrid, depending on campus — high, but not approaching Medicine's illustrative 11.4–13.4
range. As with Medicine, private universities plausibly run separate ADE/Business admissions
processes with their own tests/interviews, but this was not independently verified for Business
specifically this pass — flagged as an assumption-by-analogy, not a confirmed finding.

### Turkey — numerically stricter only, confirmed

Confirmed directly: İktisat (Economics) and İşletme (Business Administration) are placed via
YKS's **EA** (Eşit Ağırlık/Equal-Weight) score type — the same score-type cluster the existing
Turkey country doc already names for Law, Economics, Business, Psychology, and Social Work —
computed from the identical (TYT × 0.40) + (AYT × 0.60) + (OBP × 0.12 or 0.06) national formula
and placed by the identical centralized rank-order algorithm as every other lisans programme.
No dedicated test, interview, portfolio, or other non-score element exists for
Economics/Business anywhere in the domestic pathway — consistent with the existing country
doc's own finding that "Medicine, Law, top Engineering programmes remain pure score-ranked
placement with no interview or portfolio step at all," which extends identically to top
Economics/Business programmes. What varies is purely the cutoff: **Boğaziçi University's
İşletme** (English-taught) carried a 2025 taban puanı of **495.76/500** (national success rank
approximately **578th**), an extreme but not maximum cutoff — confirming the "numerically
stricter only" pattern with a real, sourced number rather than an assumption.

### Ireland — same as general admission

Business/Commerce/Economics at Irish universities (Trinity Business School, UCD's business
college, and others) was not found to carry any dedicated test, interview, or portfolio
requirement within the domestic CAO/points route — it runs through the same Leaving
Certificate points mechanism (best 6 subjects plus any Mathematics bonus) as the large majority
of Irish undergraduate courses, a sharp contrast to Medicine's nationally-uniform HPAT-plus-
points formula or Art & Design's portfolio-based "Restricted" category already documented in
this package. Subject prerequisites apply the ordinary way (commonly a named Higher Level
Mathematics minimum). The separate non-EU/direct-application route already documented generally
for Ireland (essay- and reference-bearing) would apply to a Business applicant the same way it
applies to any other non-EU-route programme — not a Business-specific overlay.

### Australia — same as general admission

Business/Commerce at Go8 and other Australian universities was not found to carry any dedicated
admissions test, interview, or government funding-mechanism exception — it runs through the
same floating ATAR/selection-rank mechanism as the large majority of Australian undergraduate
programmes. This is a sharp contrast to Medicine's confirmed exception: Commonwealth Supported
Place funding for Business, as for most Australian fields, is allocated to universities in
dollars (not fixed government-set place-counts the way Medicine's CSP allocation is), meaning
Business's capacity constraint operates entirely through ordinary ATAR-cutoff competition rather
than through a categorically different funding mechanism. No UCAT-ANZ/ISAT-style dedicated test
or Medicine-style structured interview was found for standard-entry Business/Commerce.

### New Zealand — same as general admission

Business/Commerce is not among the programmes New Zealand's existing country doc identifies as
restricted (Medicine, Pharmacy, Nursing, Engineering, and some Law specifically) — it was not
found to carry any dedicated test, interview, or gateway-year mechanism, and instead runs
through the ordinary single-stage, graduated Rank Score threshold already documented as the
general New Zealand undergraduate admission model. This entry relies more heavily on the
existing country doc's own general findings than on fresh Business-specific verification this
pass.

### Switzerland — same as general admission, an instructive near-miss

This resolves the lead directly, and the answer is a genuinely informative near-miss.
St. Gallen's distinctive foreign-applicant selection procedure (a computer-based online
aptitude test, a roughly-10-minute recorded video interview, and an English-taught Assessment
Year) — already documented in the existing Switzerland country doc as triggered by the
law-mandated 25% foreign-student quota — is confirmed directly on HSG's own admissions pages to
apply **identically** across all of HSG's programmes: Economics, Business, Law, and
International Affairs alike, gated by nationality/certificate-origin, not by academic field. A
Law applicant to HSG faces the literally identical procedure as an Economics applicant. Because
St. Gallen is itself an almost entirely business/economics/law-focused institution, most
students encountering this mechanism *are* headed toward Business/Economics — but the mechanism
itself is not a field-specific gate, it is a general-HSG, nationality-triggered one, exactly the
distinction the lead asked to verify. Separately, at Switzerland's other major
Business/Economics faculties — University of Zurich, HEC Lausanne, HEC Genève — no dedicated
numerus clausus, aptitude test, or quota comparable to St. Gallen's was found in sources
reviewed this pass; these appear to follow the general Swiss threshold-based Matura-equivalence
model already documented for most non-Medicine Swiss Bachelor's fields, though this was not
exhaustively confirmed against each institution's own primary admissions pages.

### Singapore — same as general admission, sharply contrasting with Medicine/Law in the same country

This sharply contrasts with this package's own Medicine finding for Singapore (structurally
different, via a ranking-position gate plus a mandatory standardized-test layer for
Medicine/Dentistry/Law specifically). Business is not named among NUS's ranking-position-gated
programmes — NUS's **BBA** instead runs through the same general holistic mechanism confirmed
for most NUS programmes: up to four listed achievements/co-curricular activities plus
character-limited short-response questions. **SMU** — itself an almost entirely
business/management-focused Autonomous University, structurally comparable to how St. Gallen
relates to Switzerland — runs its own general admissions mechanism (a 1,000-word essay, CCA
record, test scores, and a shortlisted small-group interview assessing listening/reasoning
rather than "correct" answers) for essentially all of its applicants, since SMU has few
programmes outside business/management/economics/law/computing/social science — this is SMU's
general institutional model, not a Business-specific overlay layered on top of something
lighter. Neither NUS Business nor SMU was found to add a mechanism *beyond* what general
admission at that AU already provides, unlike Medicine/Dentistry/Law's confirmed additional
ranking-gate-plus-test layer.

### Hong Kong — same as general admission, sharply contrasting with Medicine in the same country

As for every internationally-educated applicant in this package's Hong Kong research, JUPAS is
categorically closed, so a Turkish or other foreign-qualification Business applicant sits in
the non-JUPAS pathway by definition. Within non-JUPAS, **HKU Business School** was not found to
carry a mandatory interview the way HKU Medicine's Multiple Mini Interview format is — HKU
Business's own materials confirm an interview is **optional** and arranged only for shortlisted
applicants (individual Skype/Zoom format), a meaningfully lighter mechanism than Medicine's
compulsory MMI. A personal statement is confirmed as an important input to HKU Business's
admission decision, and references are also confirmed as one of the sources of information HKU
Business draws on — notable because recommendations are largely absent elsewhere in Hong Kong's
system, making HKU Business one of the few confirmed exceptions in this package. None of this
amounts to a dedicated new mechanism beyond the general non-JUPAS evaluation toolkit (transcript
review, personal statement, optional interview, references) already available to any non-JUPAS
programme — a sharp contrast to Medicine's confirmed mandatory-interview-plus-explicit-ceiling
finding for the same country.

## What determines eligibility

Where Economics/Business is structurally different, eligibility stacks in a way the general
country model does not otherwise require: (1) the underlying secondary qualification meeting
that country's general higher-education entry bar, exactly as for any other programme; (2) at
the elite-tier institutions/pathways specifically — a named business school's own freshman
gate (US), a qualifying TMUA/TARA score (UK, at the specific universities that require one), a
numerus-fixus selection-test result (Netherlands, at the specific capped tracks), the CPGE
ECG/ECT dossier and eventual BCE/Ecricome concours result (France), or a competitive score on a
rubric-scored video/written supplementary application (Canada, at the specific programmes that
use one). Where Economics/Business is merely numerically stricter, eligibility is exactly the
same fact the general country model already establishes, just ranked against a higher emergent
cutoff (Italy's local TOLC-Economics ranking, Germany's local-NC threshold, Spain's nota de
corte, Turkey's EA-type YKS score). Where it runs through general admission unchanged (Ireland,
Australia, New Zealand, Switzerland outside St. Gallen, Singapore, Hong Kong), eligibility is
simply whatever the country's general admissions architecture document already establishes —
this document adds nothing on top for those six.

## What genuinely strengthens an application

At the elite-tier structural-difference pathways, the highest-leverage lever is preparation for
and performance on that pathway's own dedicated instrument: a strong TMUA/TARA score at the
UK's test-requiring universities, a strong Dutch numerus-fixus selection-test result, sustained
two-year academic performance across the CPGE ECG/ECT coefficient structure culminating in
concours performance in France, and — genuinely distinct from every other lever in this
document — real-time, reflective performance on Canada's rubric-scored video/written
supplementary applications, where a thin activities list attached to a strong, specific,
reflective response can outperform a long list attached to a generic one. At the US's
direct-admit business schools, demonstrated quantitative rigor and articulated fit with a
specific named major (not "business" as a generic interest) is the clearest lever. Where the
family runs numerically stricter only, the lever is simply a higher score on the same
instrument the general country model already uses — nothing programme-specific to prepare for
beyond that. Where the family runs through general admission unchanged, whatever the general
country document already identifies as strengthening any application applies unmodified.

## What is largely irrelevant

General extracurricular breadth unconnected to demonstrated quantitative/business-adjacent
depth is not a meaningful lever anywhere Economics/Business was researched this pass — even at
Canada's video/written-supplementary-application schools, what is scored is a structured,
reflective response to a specific prompt, not an inventory of activities. Personal statements
and recommendation letters are largely or entirely absent as Economics/Business-specific levers
in Italy, Germany, Spain's public track, Turkey's domestic pathway, and Ireland's CAO/points
route — consistent with each of those countries' general-admission pattern already found
elsewhere in this package, not a Business-specific finding. At Singapore's NUS Business and
Hong Kong's HKU Business specifically, the additional standardized-test layers and mandatory
interview formats confirmed for Medicine/Dentistry/Law in the same two countries do **not**
apply — treating NUS Business or HKU Business as carrying the same added-mechanism weight as
those countries' Medicine findings would overstate their selectivity mechanism, even where
outcome selectivity is still high.

## What ORYN must never infer

Never present any single country's Economics/Business mechanism as representative of "how
Business admission works" generally — the mixed 5/4/6 split in this document is itself the
evidence against a universal model, and even within a single "structurally different" country
the mechanism is often institution- or track-specific rather than universal (the US's three
named business schools use three different mechanisms; the UK's TMUA and TARA are different
tests for different universities' Economics-adjacent courses; the Netherlands' numerus fixus
status can differ between two language tracks of the identical programme). Never assume a
field-dominant institution's general admissions mechanism (St. Gallen, SMU) is a
Business-specific gate merely because most of its applicants happen to be pursuing
Business/Economics — check whether the same mechanism applies to that institution's other
fields before concluding it is field-specific. Never assume Canada's rubric-scored video/
written supplementary-application model is a single-school (Queen's) idiosyncrasy — it recurs
at multiple leading Canadian Commerce programmes, though not universally (McGill Desautels
shows no confirmed equivalent). Never assume a recently-changed US policy (Kelley's fall-2026
holistic-review shift, Ross's fall-2025 First-Year-Admitting-Unit status) is a long-settled
mechanism rather than an active, dated change that should be reconfirmed each cycle. Never
assume France's CPGE-ECG/ECT-to-concours pathway is a subset of engineering's CPGE-to-concours
pathway — they are structurally parallel but run through entirely separate prépa tracks and
non-overlapping concours consortia (BCE/Ecricome vs. Mines-Ponts/Centrale-Supélec/e3a/X-ENS),
leading to different named schools.

## Institution-specific vs. programme-specific vs. country-wide patterns

**Country-wide, uniform**: Turkey's EA-score-type mechanism for Economics/Business (uniform
nationally, set by ÖSYM); Spain's nota de acceso/nota de admisión formula (uniform nationally,
administered regionally); Germany's exclusion of Business/Economics from the national NC layer
(uniform — national NC covers exactly four subjects, never Business); Ireland's CAO/points
route carrying no Business-specific overlay (uniform across the EU/EFTA/UK-fee-status
population).

**Programme/track-specific within one country**: the Netherlands' UvA Economics and Business
Economics open-Dutch-track-vs-capped-English-track split (the same programme, same university,
differing purely by language track); France's CPGE-ECG/ECT-and-concours-vs-ordinary-Licence
split; the UK's TMUA-vs-TARA split by which university's Economics-adjacent course a student
targets; Italy's locally-capped-vs-open TOLC-Economics split, which varies by university and
year with no nationally uniform list.

**Genuinely institution-specific, varying even within one field in one country**: the United
States' three named direct-admit business schools (McCombs, Kelley, Ross), each confirmed to
use a materially different admissions mechanism and each independently changing on its own
timeline; Canada's Commerce-programme supplementary-application model, confirmed at four
leading programmes but not at a fifth (McGill Desautels); Switzerland's St. Gallen-vs-everywhere-
else split, which is really an HSG-specific fact (the foreign-quota mechanism) rather than a
Business-specific one; Hong Kong's HKU Business optional-interview-plus-references pattern,
not independently confirmed at HKUST or CUHK; Singapore's NUS-Business-vs-SMU split, where both
run through their own institution's general mechanism rather than a shared Business-specific
one.

The practical implication for ORYN: an "Economics/Business requirement" data model needs, even
more than Medicine's did, an explicit field for whether a given fact describes the *general
field* in that country or only its *elite inner track* — collapsing "France requires a
competitive concours for Economics/Business" into a country-wide fact would be as wrong as
